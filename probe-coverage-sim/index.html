How the model works, and what it leaves out
  • The galaxy is a flat disk of about 120 billion stars, divided into squares 1,000 light-years across. Odds are worked out for the real number of stars in each square; the dots on the map are only scenery.
  • Probes fired in random directions use full three-dimensional geometry. A probe launched from r light-years away passes within d of a particular star with probability d² / 4r², which is why sheer numbers of probes achieve so little.
  • Aimed probes are shared evenly among the stars within range, or among life-bearing stars first if you choose that.
  • Self-replicators spread from square to square. Their speed comes from the probe speed, a 7-light-year hop between neighbouring stars and the pause to build copies. Each link between squares succeeds with the reliability you set, which makes the spread a percolation process.
  • Light travels at its own speed, so a civilisation is in our sky only while the light from its active years is arriving here.
  • Stars don’t move in this model. In reality the Sun circles the galaxy roughly every 230 million years, which shuffles neighbourhoods and generally helps probes reach more systems.
  • A visit means a probe passed by or arrived. Whether it stayed, left anything behind, or could be spotted today is a separate question.
